Key takeaways

  • In a closed tank, the transmitter HP side connects to the bottom and the LP side to the vapour space.
  • Vessel pressure acts on both sides and cancels, so the DP is set by liquid height and density.
  • Use a dry leg when the vapour does not condense; use a wet leg when it does.
  • A wet leg adds a fixed head on the LP side, which makes the calibrated range negative.

A level transmitter on an open tank only has to read the liquid head. In a pressurised or vented vapour space, however, the vessel pressure adds to the reading and must be removed. DP level measurement solves this by measuring the difference between the bottom of the tank and the vapour space above the liquid.

How DP Level Measurement Works in a Closed Tank

The hydrostatic pressure of a liquid is its height times its density. In a closed tank, the bottom connection sees that head plus the vessel pressure, while the top connection sees the vessel pressure alone. As Yokogawa explains, the low pressure side of the transmitter is connected to the top of the tank, so the pressure at the tank top becomes the reference. The vessel pressure cancels, and the output, 4 mA at empty and 20 mA at full, follows level only.

Dry Leg vs Wet Leg

Dry leg

  • LP impulse line is filled with gas or air
  • For vapour that does not condense
  • Process temperature close to ambient
  • LP side adds no liquid head

Wet leg

  • LP impulse line is filled with a liquid
  • For vapour that tends to condense
  • Process temperature well above or below ambient
  • Fixed head on the LP side shifts the range

The choice follows the guidance from Instrumentation Tools. If condensate collects in a dry leg, it adds an unknown head on the LP side and the level reading drifts low, which is why a condensing service needs a deliberately filled wet leg instead.

Calculating LRV and URV

The lower range value (LRV) is the DP at the zero level and the upper range value (URV) is the DP at the maximum level. With H1 as the span, H2 as the height of the zero level above the HP tap, SG1 as the liquid specific gravity and, for a wet leg, Y as the wet leg height and SG2 as its fill liquid gravity:

Dry leg: LRV = H2 × SG1 and URV = (H1 + H2) × SG1.
Wet leg: LRV = H2 × SG1 minus Y × SG2 and URV = (H1 + H2) × SG1 minus Y × SG2.

Enter the LRV and URV in the transmitter with a HART communicator. The DP level range calculator and the 4 to 20 mA scaling calculator help you check the numbers.

Did you know?

In the wet leg example, both the LRV and the URV are negative. With the tank full, the liquid head on the HP side (630 mmWC) is still smaller than the 700 mmWC head of the wet leg, so the transmitter sees more pressure on its LP side across the whole range.

Remote Seals and Capillaries

Remote seals connected by filled capillaries are one of the most common ways to connect a DP transmitter, and Yokogawa notes that they eliminate corrosion, heat and maintenance issues of impulse lines. The fill fluid adds its own head, which the transmitter can compensate for, but temperature changes the fill density. Unequal capillary lengths or sun on one side cause drift, so balanced systems use equal capillaries or matched fill volumes. Electronic remote sensors replace capillaries with wiring, although accuracy is harder to achieve at high static pressure.

Installation Checklist

  1. Connections. HP to the bottom tap, LP to the vapour space above the maximum level.
  2. Leg type. Dry leg for non condensing vapour, wet leg for condensing service.
  3. Isolation. Fit a valve manifold from our manifolds and fittings range for safe zeroing.
  4. Ranging. Calculate LRV and URV from the real tap heights and fill liquid gravity.
  5. Verification. Check the reading at a known level after commissioning.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Dry leg on condensing vapour. Condensate builds up in the LP line and the reading drifts.
Wrong wet leg gravity. An incorrect SG2 shifts both range values.
Unbalanced capillaries. Different lengths or temperatures on each side cause zero drift.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is DP level measurement used on closed tanks?

Because the LP side senses the vessel pressure and cancels it, leaving only the liquid head.

When should I use a wet leg instead of a dry leg?

Use a wet leg when the vapour tends to condense, for example when the process is well above ambient temperature.

Why are the range values negative with a wet leg?

The filled LP leg adds a fixed head; when that head is larger than the liquid head, the DP stays below zero across the range.
