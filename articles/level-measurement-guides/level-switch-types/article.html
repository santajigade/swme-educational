Key takeaways

  • A level switch detects whether product is present at one set height and gives an on or off signal.
  • Float, vibrating fork, conductive, capacitive, rotary paddle and optical switches are the most common types.
  • The right type depends on the medium: liquid or solid, conductive or not, clean, foaming or sticky.
  • Switches are used for high and low alarms, pump protection and overfill prevention.

Not every tank needs a continuous level reading. Often the process only needs to know whether the level has reached a certain point, for example to stop a pump before it runs dry. That is the job of a level switch, a point level device that changes state when product reaches its sensing position.

What a Level Switch Does

A level switch is a digital input: it tells the control system that the level is above or below one point. It typically triggers a high or low alarm or a process action, such as starting or stopping a pump. For example, when the level in a suction tank falls below the low switch, the input turns off and the PLC stops the pump, which protects it from running dry.

Level Switch Types

{{infographic}}

Float switch

A float rides on the liquid surface and operates a mechanical or reed switch at a set position. In vertical designs, a magnetic float slides on a stem and operates a reed switch inside it. Suspended cable floats tilt to change state and are simple and popular for pump protection, though their switching point is less precise. Displacer switches work in a similar way, using buoyancy on a heavy displacer hung from a spring.

Vibrating fork switch

A piezoelectric drive makes a fork vibrate at its resonant frequency. When product covers the fork, the vibration changes and the switch changes state. Vibrating fork switches suit liquids and granular solids, and they are largely unaffected by viscosity, conductivity, foam and pressure. They need no calibration and little maintenance.

Conductive switch

Two or more electrodes sit in an open circuit. When a conductive liquid such as water, acid or a water based solution bridges them, current flows and the switch operates. Conductive switches are simple and inexpensive, and multi electrode versions can detect several levels. However, they do not work on hydrocarbons, solvents or oils.

Capacitive switch

The probe and the tank wall form a capacitor. When product covers the probe, the capacitance changes and the electronics switch the output. Capacitive switches work on both conductive and non conductive liquids and solids. For an example, read our blog on the Gems ULS 200 capacitive level sensor.

Rotary paddle and optical switches

A rotary paddle switch uses a slow motor to turn a paddle. When bulk solids such as cement, grain or sugar stop the paddle, the switch changes state, which makes it a simple choice for bins, hoppers and silos. An optical switch sends infrared light into a prism; when the tip is in liquid, less light returns to the receiver. Optical switches have no moving parts and need no calibration.

Did you know?

A conductive level switch that works perfectly in a water tank will never switch in a diesel tank. Oils and hydrocarbons have very low conductivity, so no current can flow between the electrodes. For these liquids, choose a float, vibrating fork, capacitive or optical switch instead.

How to Choose a Level Switch

  1. Medium. Is it a liquid, slurry or bulk solid?
  2. Electrical properties. Conductive liquids allow conductive switches; oils need another type.
  3. Process conditions. Check pressure, temperature, foam, viscosity and buildup.
  4. Function. Define each point: high alarm, low alarm, pump start or pump stop.
  5. Mounting. Top, side or cable mounting, and the space available inside the vessel.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Conductive switch on hydrocarbons. Oils and solvents do not conduct, so the switch never operates.
Too light a displacer. The displacer must be heavier than the medium in normal conditions, or the weight change cannot be detected.
Liquid switches in silos. Float and conductive switches are made for liquids; bulk solids need a paddle, fork or capacitive switch.
Paddle switch on complex duties. Rotary paddles suit simple bulk solids applications; harder duties need another technology.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a level switch and a level transmitter?

A level switch gives an on or off signal at one point, while a level transmitter gives a continuous reading across the whole range.

Which level switch works on both liquids and solids?

Vibrating fork and capacitive switches can be used on both liquids and bulk solids.

Can a float switch protect a pump from running dry?

Yes. A low level float switch is a common way to stop a pump before the tank empties.
