Key takeaways

  • A control valve is the most common final control element: it changes the size of the flow passage as the controller asks.
  • The body holds the pressure, the trim (plug, seat ring, cage and stem) shapes the flow, and the actuator supplies the force.
  • An air to open valve fails closed on loss of air, and an air to close valve fails open.
  • Emerson advises sizing for about 60 to 80% open at maximum flow and not less than 20% open at minimum flow.

Emerson's Fisher control valve handbook calls the control valve the most common final control element in the process industries, and defines it as a device that modulates flow by varying the size of the flow passage as directed by a signal from a controller. How a Control Valve Works in the Loop

The controller output is usually 4 to 20 mA, while most actuators run on air. An I/P converter turns the current into air pressure; ABB lists 0/4 to 20 mA in and 0.2 to 1 bar or 3 to 15 psi out. Emerson gives 20 psig as a common supply pressure for a 3 to 15 psig range. A positioner is a position controller mechanically linked to the valve stem, which Emerson describes as a high proportional gain device. Its job is to make sure the stem goes where the signal asks.

Body Styles

Emerson divides bodies into two groups. Sliding stem valves use linear motion to move a closure member into and out of a seating surface, and the globe valve is the usual example. Rotary valves turn the closure member instead: full or segmented ball, butterfly disc and eccentric plug designs. Emerson notes segmented ball bodies give rangeability in excess of 300:1. Trim and Flow Characteristics

Trim parts

Trim: The internal parts that modulate the flow (Emerson).
Plug: The closure member in a sliding stem valve.
Seat ring: Gives the closure member its seating surface.
Cage: Surrounds the closure member and can characterise the flow or provide the seat.
Stem: Connects the actuator stem to the closure member.

Actuators and Fail Action

Emerson defines an actuator as a pneumatic, hydraulic or electric device that supplies force and motion to open or close a valve. The guide to control valve actuators explains the main types:

Pneumatic

  • Diaphragm actuators use a large coil spring for the default position
  • Piston actuators generally have longer strokes than diaphragm types
  • Double acting actuators tend to stay in position on air failure

Electric and hydraulic

  • Most electric actuators use a worm gear to cut motor speed
  • Electric valves usually stay in their last position on power loss
  • Nearly all hydraulic designs use a piston, some with their own pump

The valve failure mode lesson sums up fail action: an air to open valve fails closed on loss of air pressure, and an air to close valve fails open.

Did you know?

In a control valve the positioner, not the actuator spring alone, decides where the stem stops. Emerson describes it as a servomechanism: it compares the signal with the actual travel and adjusts the air until they match.

Cv and Kv

Cv is the number of US gallons per minute of 60 °F water that flows through a valve with a 1 psi pressure drop. Kv is the flow in m³/h of water at 5 to 30 °C with a 1 bar drop. Project Materials gives Cv = 1.156 × Kv and Kv = 0.865 × Cv, and Spirax Sarco gives the more exact factor 1.156 099. Sizing a Control Valve

  1. Size for the right travel. Emerson's valve sizing article says a valve controls best at about 60 to 80% open at maximum flow and not less than 20% open at minimum flow.
  2. Avoid oversizing. The same article says an oversized equal percentage valve throttling at the low end can fall to a turndown of 3:1 or less.
  3. Allow enough pressure drop. A rule of thumb from The Chemical Engineer is 20% of the total system drop or 10 psi (0.7 bar), whichever is greater.
  4. Check for cavitation and flashing. Emerson says choked flow is cavitation if outlet pressure is above the vapour pressure, and flashing if it is below.
  5. Pick the leakage class. Match the class to how tightly the valve must shut off.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Oversizing the control valve. It throttles near its seat and loses turndown.
Picking the fail position by habit. Decide what the process needs on loss of air, then choose air to open or air to close.
Using quick opening trim for throttling. It suits on and off service.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between Cv and Kv?

Cv uses US gpm at 1 psi drop; Kv uses m³/h at 1 bar. Cv = 1.156 × Kv.

What does air to open mean?

Air pressure opens the valve and the spring closes it, so it fails closed when air is lost.

What does a valve positioner do?

It is a position controller linked to the stem. Emerson calls it a high gain device that keeps the stem where the signal asks.
