A pressure transmitter is a field instrument that measures the pressure of a liquid, gas or steam and converts it into a standard electrical signal. The control system, usually a PLC or DCS, reads this signal to monitor the process, run control loops and trigger alarms. What a Pressure Transmitter Does

A pressure gauge shows pressure to an operator standing next to it. A pressure transmitter sends the same information to a control room, many metres or even kilometres away. This allows the plant to record trends, close control loops and shut down equipment safely when limits are exceeded.

For example, a transmitter on a pump discharge line can tell the control system that pressure is rising because a downstream valve has closed. The system can then reduce pump speed or stop the pump before the line is overpressured. In addition, the same signal can be logged for maintenance teams to study later.

At a glance

4 to 20 mA: the standard analog output used in most plants.
0.04 to 0.5%: the typical accuracy range, depending on the model.
Two wire: loop powered, so power and signal share one cable pair.

Did you know?

The 4 to 20 mA standard starts at 4 mA instead of 0 mA for a reason. This live zero lets the system tell the difference between a true zero reading and a broken cable. Gauge, Absolute and Differential Pressure

Before you choose a pressure transmitter, decide which pressure reference your process needs.

Gauge pressure: measured relative to the surrounding atmosphere. It is used for most pipelines, pumps and vessels.
Absolute pressure: measured relative to a perfect vacuum. It is used for vacuum systems, distillation and barometric work.
Differential pressure: the difference between two points. It is used for flow, level and filter monitoring.

Output Signals and Communication

The 4 to 20 mA current loop remains the industry standard. It resists electrical noise over long cable runs, and a reading of 0 mA immediately shows a broken wire.

The HART protocol lets a technician change the range, read diagnostics and check the sensor without opening the loop. This saves time during commissioning and maintenance.

How to Select a Pressure Transmitter

A careful selection prevents most field problems. Work through these points for every pressure transmitter you specify.

  1. Pressure type and range. Choose gauge, absolute or differential. Select a range where normal pressure sits in the middle third of the span.
  2. Overpressure limit. Check that pressure spikes and water hammer stay below the rated overload.
  3. Process temperature. For hot, viscous or crystallizing media, use diaphragm seals with a capillary or cooling element.
  4. Wetted materials. Match the diaphragm and connection to the medium, for example 316L, Hastelloy, Monel or ceramic.
  5. Accuracy and stability. Consider total probable error, including temperature effect and long term drift, not only reference accuracy.
  6. Output and protocol. Confirm what your control system expects, such as 4 to 20 mA with HART.
  7. Hazardous area approval. In classified zones, specify IECEx or ATEX certification with the correct protection method.
  8. Connections and enclosure. Select the process connection, cable entry and IP rating to suit the site.

Pressure Transmitter Installation Best Practice

Liquid service

  • Mount the transmitter below the tapping point.
  • Trapped gas then rises back into the process.
  • Fill impulse lines completely before start up.

Gas service

  • Mount the transmitter above the tapping point.
  • Condensate then drains back into the process.
  • Slope impulse lines continuously toward the tap.

Use a valve manifold so you can isolate, vent and check zero without removing the instrument. SWME supplies manifolds and fittings for single and differential transmitters. In addition, keep impulse lines short and protect them from freezing or overheating.

Finally, verify every transmitter regularly against a reference. Our test and calibration equipment range supports field checks without returning the instrument to a workshop.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Oversized range. A 0 to 100 bar unit on a 5 bar process loses resolution and accuracy.
Wrong mounting position. Gas pockets in liquid lines cause noisy and offset readings.
Ignoring temperature. Hot media without a seal or cooling element shortens sensor life.
No overpressure protection. Water hammer can permanently damage the diaphragm.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a pressure transmitter and a pressure transducer?

A transducer converts pressure into a raw electrical signal, often in millivolts. A transmitter adds electronics that condition this signal into a standard output such as 4 to 20 mA.

How often should a pressure transmitter be calibrated?

Most plants check critical transmitters every 12 to 24 months. Safety loops often need shorter intervals, as defined by your site procedures.

Can a pressure transmitter measure level?

Yes. It can calculate liquid level from hydrostatic pressure when the density is known. See this guide to DP transmitter level measurement.
