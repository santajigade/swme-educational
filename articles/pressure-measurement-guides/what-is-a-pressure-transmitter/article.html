A pressure transmitter is an instrument that measures the pressure of a liquid, gas or steam and converts it into a standard electrical signal that a control system can read. It is one of the most widely used field instruments in Oil and Gas, Petrochemical, Water and Utilities plants.

How a Pressure Transmitter Works

Process pressure acts on a thin diaphragm. The diaphragm deflects by a very small amount, and a sensing element converts this movement into an electrical change. The transmitter electronics then amplify, linearize and temperature compensate that change and send it out as a standard signal, usually 4 to 20 mA.

Gauge, Absolute and Differential Pressure

  • Gauge pressure is measured relative to the surrounding atmosphere.
  • Absolute pressure is measured relative to a perfect vacuum.
  • Differential pressure is the difference between two process points, used for flow, level and filter monitoring.

Output Signals

The 4 to 20 mA loop remains the industry standard because it is robust against electrical noise and detects a broken wire (0 mA). Smart transmitters add HART communication on top of the analog signal for remote configuration and diagnostics. Fieldbus versions such as Foundation Fieldbus and PROFIBUS PA are used in fully digital plants.

How to Select a Pressure Transmitter

  1. Define the pressure type and range, with the normal value in the middle third of the range.
  2. Check the process temperature and choose diaphragm seals for hot, viscous or crystallizing media.
  3. Match wetted materials to the medium, for example 316L, Hastelloy or ceramic.
  4. Select the output signal and communication protocol required by your control system.
  5. Confirm hazardous area approvals such as ATEX or IECEx where needed.
  6. Choose the process connection and electrical connection to suit the installation.

Installation Tips

  • For liquids, mount the transmitter below the tapping point so trapped gas can escape.
  • For gases, mount it above the tapping point so condensate drains back to the process.
  • Use a manifold for isolation, venting and zero checks without removing the instrument.
