Key takeaways

  • A pressure switch opens or closes an electrical contact when process pressure reaches a set value.
  • Electromechanical switches use a diaphragm, piston, bellows or bourdon tube against a spring and need no external power.
  • Deadband is the gap between the trip point and the reset point; it stops the contacts from chattering.
  • Select by range, setpoint position, overpressure, wetted materials, contact rating and approvals.

Many control and protection functions only need to know when pressure crosses one value. A pressure switch does exactly that: it senses pressure and mechanically opens or closes an electrical circuit at a set point. Pressure switches are used with pumps, boilers, burners, compressors and filters wherever a pressure change must trigger an electrical response.

How a Pressure Switch Works

In an electromechanical design, process pressure acts on a sensing element against an opposing range spring. An adjusting nut sets the spring force, which sets the trip point. Only when the pressure force exceeds the spring force does the element move, pushing a rod that changes the state of a snap acting microswitch. According to Ashcroft, raising the adjusting nut reduces the spring force and lowers the setpoint, while lowering it raises the setpoint.

Sensing elements

Diaphragm: Moves with pressure and passes the force to the switch. Material must suit the medium; Ashcroft lists elastomer diaphragms up to 3000 psi (210 bar) and stainless steel up to 1000 psi (70 bar).
Piston: Used for higher pressure designs, generally 1,000 psi and above. Piston switches handle surges and spikes best.
Bourdon tube or bellows: A tube or bellows deflects with pressure and operates the switch; bourdon tube switches typically have narrow deadbands.

Contacts and Setpoint

A normally open (NO) contact is open below the setpoint and closes above it. A normally closed (NC) contact is closed below the setpoint and opens above it. For vacuum switches the behaviour is reversed. An SPDT microswitch has one NO, one NC and one common terminal, while a DPDT version uses two SPDT switches that actuate together, so two separate loads switch at the same time.

Where the setpoint sits in the range matters. AutomationDirect advises placing it in the middle of the range for electromechanical switches, to balance accuracy and switch life, and in the upper 25% of the range for solid state switches.

Deadband Explained

Deadband, also called differential or hysteresis, is the difference between the pressure at which the switch trips and the pressure at which it resets. On a rising setpoint the switch resets at a lower pressure; on a falling setpoint it resets at a higher pressure. As Omega explains, deadband prevents rapid switching that would disturb the process and wear the contacts.

As a rule, use a narrow deadband for alarms and a wider one for control duties, such as starting and stopping a pump. Bourdon tube and diaphragm switches typically have narrow deadbands, while piston switches have broad ones.

Did you know?

An electromechanical pressure switch needs no external power. Its sensing element moves the contacts directly, so it can switch loads rated at 3 to 15 amps and 120 to 480 VAC. A solid state switch, by contrast, typically needs a 24 VDC supply.

How to Select a Pressure Switch

  1. Pressure type. Gauge, absolute or differential; see gauge vs absolute vs differential pressure.
  2. Range and setpoint. Choose a range that puts the setpoint in the recommended part of the scale.
  3. Overpressure. Check proof and burst pressure; Ashcroft states burst pressure is typically at least 400% of range. Add a snubber from our gauge accessories where spikes occur.
  4. Wetted materials. Match diaphragm, piston and O ring materials to the medium and temperature.
  5. Electrical. Contact type (SPDT or DPDT), rating and hazardous area approvals; read pressure measurement in hazardous areas.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Setpoint at the edge of the range. Accuracy and switch life both suffer; follow the recommended position.
Ignoring deadband. Too narrow a deadband on a pump control duty makes the pump start and stop too often.
Wrong contact logic. Confirm NO or NC with a multimeter before wiring an alarm or trip.
No surge protection. Pressure spikes beyond proof pressure can permanently damage the element.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a pressure switch and a pressure transmitter?

A pressure switch gives an on or off contact at one setpoint, while a transmitter gives a continuous signal across its whole range.

What is deadband on a pressure switch?

It is the difference between the trip pressure and the reset pressure, which prevents the contacts from chattering.

Does a mechanical pressure switch need power?

No. The sensing element operates the contacts directly; only solid state switches need an external supply.
