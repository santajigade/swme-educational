How an Infrared Thermometer Works

DwyerOmega describes the basic design as a lens that focuses infrared energy onto a detector, which turns it into an electrical signal. The signal is compensated for ambient temperature and shown as a temperature. Its infrared thermometer guide covers the optics in more detail.

Fluke's infrared temperature calibration primer says the most common spectral band for general purpose infrared thermometers is 8 to 14 µm. The optics filter the incoming energy to that band.

Emissivity

Fluke defines emissivity as the ratio of the radiant energy a surface emits to the energy a blackbody emits at the same temperature. A blackbody has an emissivity of 1.0 and real surfaces fall between 0 and 1. Smooth metals have low emissivity and high reflectivity, so an infrared thermometer pointed at them picks up reflected energy from the room as well.

Fluke shows how much this matters: a 1% error in emissivity at 500 °C gives a 3.5 °C error in the calibration. Simple instruments have a Low setting for shiny metals and a High setting for wood, paint and plastic. Others let you adjust the value, which the methods below rely on.

Four ways to find emissivity (DwyerOmega)

Tape: Stick masking tape (emissivity 0.95) on the surface, measure the tape, and set the emissivity so a reading beside it matches. DwyerOmega limits this to about 500 °F (260 °C).
Paint: Coat a small area with dull black paint, close to 1.0, and use it the same way.
Drilled hole: At high temperature, drill a hole at least 6 times as deep as its diameter. It acts as a blackbody.
Known temperature: Heat a sample of the material to a known temperature and adjust emissivity until the instrument reads it.

Distance to Spot Ratio

The distance to spot ratio, written D:S, tells you how large an area the instrument averages over. Fluke's example: a 12:1 ratio means you measure a 1 inch spot from 12 inches away. Step back and the spot grows. DwyerOmega says the target should completely fill the field of view. Otherwise the instrument averages the target with whatever is behind it.

The laser dot does not show the measured area. Fluke stresses that it is only an aiming aid. On a small pipe, move closer until the pipe is wider than the spot.

Did you know?

Fluke notes that glass is transparent to visible light but opaque to infrared longer than about 3 µm. An 8 to 14 µm thermometer pointed through a window reads the glass, not what is behind it.

What Disturbs the Reading

Between you and the target

  • Steam, dust and smoke scatter infrared energy (Fluke).
  • Glass and inspection windows block the 8 to 14 µm band.
  • Fluke also warns about dirty or scratched lenses.

At the target or the instrument

  • Shiny metal reflects energy from walls and windows, so the instrument may under report.
  • A very cold target in a warm room is hard to read (Fluke).
  • Below the dew point, ice can form on the target and change its emissivity.

Getting a Good Reading

  1. Let the instrument settle. Fluke suggests leaving it in its working environment for about 30 minutes before critical readings, and avoiding sudden temperature changes.
  2. Set the emissivity. Use the tape, paint or reference methods above on shiny or unknown surfaces.
  3. Fill the spot. Work out the spot size from the D:S ratio and your distance, and make sure the target is larger.
  4. Keep the path clear. Measure away from steam and dust, and never through glass.
  5. Calibrate against a radiation source. Fluke explains that infrared thermometers are calibrated against a blackbody cavity or flat plate source checked with a radiometer, not against a contact thermometer alone.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Reading the laser dot as the measured area. The measured spot is set by the D:S ratio and your distance, not by the dot.
Pointing at bare stainless or aluminium. Low emissivity makes the instrument under report. Use tape or paint, as Fluke's infrared thermometer tips recommend.
Measuring through a sight glass. The instrument reads the glass, which is opaque in its band.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an infrared thermometer measure the fluid inside a pipe?

No. It reads the surface it sees. For the process fluid, use a contact sensor in a thermowell.

What does a D:S ratio of 12:1 mean?

At 12 inches from the target you measure a spot 1 inch across.

Why does my infrared thermometer read low on shiny metal?

Shiny metal has low emissivity, so it emits less energy than the instrument expects. Correct the emissivity or cover the spot with tape or paint.
