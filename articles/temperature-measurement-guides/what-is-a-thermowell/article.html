Key takeaways

  • A thermowell is a closed tube that protects a temperature sensor from pressure, flow and corrosion.
  • It allows the sensor to be removed without opening the process.
  • Material, shape and immersion length affect both accuracy and safety.
  • A wake frequency calculation to ASME PTC 19.3 TW prevents flow induced failures.

A thermowell is a pressure tight tube, closed at one end, that is fitted into a pipe or vessel. The temperature sensor, such as an RTD, thermocouple or bimetal thermometer, slides inside it. The thermowell takes the process pressure and flow forces, while the sensor only needs to measure heat.

This guide explains why thermowells matter, the main types and how to size them correctly. SWME supplies thermowells together with RTD and thermocouple assemblies and temperature gauges.

Why Use a Thermowell?

Protection: the sensor is shielded from high pressure, high velocity and corrosive or abrasive media.
Maintenance: the sensor can be replaced or calibrated while the process keeps running.
Safety: the pressure boundary is a solid, machined part, not a thin sensor sheath.

For example, a steam line at 40 bar and 400 degrees Celsius would quickly destroy an unprotected sensor. With a thermowell, a technician can remove the RTD for calibration in minutes, without isolating the line.

Thermowells are also described by stem shape. Straight stems are simple; tapered stems are stiffer and respond faster; stepped stems combine a thick root with a thin tip for quick response.

Thermowell Materials

Common materials

  • 316 or 316L stainless steel for general service.
  • Carbon steel for steam and hydrocarbons where corrosion is low.
  • Inconel for high temperature and oxidising atmospheres.

Special materials

  • Hastelloy or Monel for chlorides and seawater.
  • Duplex for offshore and high strength duty.
  • PTFE coating or tantalum sheath for strong acids.

Did you know?

Thermowells are usually machined from solid bar stock, not made from welded tube. A solid bar has no seam, so it withstands much higher pressure and vibration. This is why a drilled thermowell looks heavy compared with the small sensor inside it.

Immersion Length and Response Time

The tip of the thermowell should sit in the centre third of the pipe, where the flow represents the true process temperature. If the well is too short, heat conducted along the stem from the colder pipe wall makes the reading low.

However, a thicker or longer thermowell also slows the response. A bare sensor might respond in a few seconds, while the same sensor in a heavy thermowell may take a minute or more. Spring loaded sensors and a little heat transfer paste reduce this delay. You can estimate the effect with the sensor response time calculator.

Wake Frequency Calculation

When fluid flows past a thermowell, vortices shed alternately from each side. This creates a vibrating force at the vortex shedding frequency. If that frequency approaches the natural frequency of the thermowell, the stem can resonate and break, sending pieces down the line.

The ASME PTC 19.3 TW standard defines how to check this, including frequency limits, stress and pressure ratings. The vortex shedding frequency calculator shows the basic relationship between velocity and shedding frequency. In addition, the pipe flow velocity calculator gives the velocity input.

  1. Collect process data. Fluid, velocity, density, viscosity, pressure and temperature.
  2. Define geometry. Stem shape, root and tip diameters, bore and immersion length.
  3. Run the calculation. Check frequency ratio, dynamic stress and pressure rating.
  4. Fix failures. Shorten the stem, increase root diameter or add a velocity collar if needed.

A correctly sized thermowell is especially important in high velocity gas and steam lines. For more on temperature instruments, browse the temperature measurement articles on instrumentationblog.in, or read how an RTD works.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Skipping the wake frequency check. Long, thin thermowells in fast flow can fatigue and fail.
Air gap between sensor and well. Poor contact slows response and lowers the reading.
Wrong bore size. The sensor does not fit or touches only on one side.
Installing near a mixing point. The reading fluctuates because the fluid is not fully mixed.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a thermowell affect accuracy?

It does not change the final value if the immersion length is correct, but it slows the response to temperature changes.

What standard covers thermowell design?

ASME PTC 19.3 TW is the widely used standard for thermowell stress and wake frequency calculations.

Can a thermowell be used with a temperature gauge?

Yes. Bimetal and gas filled temperature gauges are commonly installed in thermowells so they can be removed safely.
