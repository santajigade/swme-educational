Key takeaways

  • A temperature switch opens or closes an electrical contact when the temperature goes above or below a set level.
  • Mechanical types use the expansion of a bimetal, liquid or gas and need no power; electronic types read an RTD.
  • The deadband is the gap between the setpoint on rising temperature and the reset point on falling temperature.
  • A limiter stays tripped after an over temperature until someone presses reset.

A temperature switch gives a plain on or off signal at a chosen temperature: switch off a heater, start a fan on a compressor, or act as a high limit. SWME supplies temperature switches, thermowells and RTD and thermocouple sensors for plants across the UAE and GCC.

How a Temperature Switch Works

JUMO explains that mechanical switches rely on metals, liquids or gases expanding as they warm until they actuate a contact. In a filled system the probe, capillary and membrane form a sealed unit. When the probe heats up the system pressure rises, the membrane expands and a snap action switch trips.

Baumer, quoted by Instrumentation Tools, describes the same idea as a pressure switch with a sealed bulb fitted to its pressure port. The fill's vapour pressure acts on a diaphragm and piston assembly, which works a micro switch at the setpoint. Compare it with our guide to what a pressure switch is.

Mechanical switches are passive and need no supply voltage. JUMO says most handle switching currents of 10 A or more, so small heaters can be switched directly, while larger loads need a contactor. The bimetal principle is the same one used in our bimetal thermometer guide.

Setpoint and Deadband

The setpoint is where the contact changes. The reset point is where it changes back, and the gap between the two is the deadband, which JUMO calls switching hysteresis. Baumer says the setpoint must sit inside the adjustable range and be stated as rising or falling, that the deadband is normally fixed, and that the standard factory setting is 50% of the scale on falling temperature, though this varies by vendor.

Did you know?

Some switches, such as WIKA's TS 972, let you adjust the differential. Its manual lists a switch differential larger than the switch point as one reason a contact will not switch.

Contacts and Limiters

SPDT: Three terminals: common (C), normally open (NO) and normally closed (NC).
DPDT: Two SPDT elements that actuate and reset together, so two separate circuits can be switched.
Limiter: After an over temperature trip the contact does not close by itself; a reset button must be pressed. JUMO notes limiters are often used on unattended systems.
Typical duties: Heater cutouts, compressor protection with bimetal switches, and limits that JUMO says are mandatory on heat transfer oil systems and grain dryers.

JUMO also describes pairing a controller with a separate switch, so the controller regulates and the switch acts as an independent limit. For a continuous signal instead, see temperature transmitters.

Installation

  1. Orientation. WIKA's TS 972 manual says to mount vertically with the bulb fully immersed.
  2. Temperature limit. The same manual warns the sensing element is permanently damaged if its maximum temperature is exceeded even once.
  3. Thermowell. Use one for incompatible media; WIKA also recommends it above 1 bar line pressure or above boiling point. See what is a thermowell.
  4. Capillary care. Do not carry the switch by the capillary or bend it sharply at the bulb.
  5. Environment. Avoid temperature shocks, and stop ice forming in humid conditions below zero.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Ignoring the deadband. A temperature switch does not reset at its setpoint; it resets at the lower reset point.
Overloading the contact. Switch large loads through a contactor.
Overheating the bulb once. WIKA says a single excursion past the maximum can permanently damage the element.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a temperature switch need power?

Mechanical types do not. Electronic types do, because they evaluate an RTD signal.

What is the deadband?

The difference between the setpoint on rising temperature and the reset point on falling temperature.

What is the difference between a switch and a limiter?

A limiter stays tripped after an over temperature until it is reset by hand.
