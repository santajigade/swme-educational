Key takeaways

  • OSHA defines an oxygen deficient atmosphere as below 19.5% by volume and an enriched one as above 23.5%.
  • Low oxygen gives no warning, and below 10% a single breath can cause loss of consciousness.
  • Most fixed oxygen monitors alarm at 19.5%, and NIH also sets a high alarm at 23.5%.
  • For oxygen deficiency monitoring, mount sensors at the height where the released gas will collect, and calibrate at least every 12 months.

Oxygen deficiency monitoring protects people in places where nitrogen, argon, CO2 or cryogenic liquids can push the oxygen out of the air. LBNL points out that these inert gases have no colour, smell or warning properties. Oxygen Limits and Their Effects

Under OSHA 1910.146 for permit required confined spaces, an oxygen deficient atmosphere contains less than 19.5% oxygen by volume and an enriched atmosphere more than 23.5%. OSHA 1910.134 adds that all oxygen deficient atmospheres shall be considered immediately dangerous to life or health. Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory gives normal air as 20.9% at sea level, while UK HSE uses 20.8%.

Did you know?

University of Cambridge guidance warns that even a single breath at oxygen concentrations below 10% can result in loss of consciousness. The same guidance says that below 12% people can lose consciousness suddenly and without warning.

Causes of Oxygen Deficiency

Displacement: OSHA lists carbon dioxide, nitrogen and argon pushing oxygen out of a space.
Consumption: Rusting metal, combustion, bacterial activity, ripening fruit and drying paint or coatings all use up oxygen.
Cryogenic liquids: LBNL notes liquid nitrogen expands nearly 700 times when it vaporises, so a spilled 4 L Dewar can displace nearly 2,800 L.
Welding and purging: UK HSE says argon, nitrogen and CO2 shielding and purge gases can settle in tank bottoms, pits and sumps.

OSHA's shipyard guidance calls oxygen deficient atmospheres the leading cause of confined space deaths in shipyards. NIH requires oxygen monitoring in freezer rooms and rooms where cryogenic fluids are supplied, and lists MRI and NMR rooms, tank farms and compressed gas manifold rooms. The oxygen deficiency hazard notes from LBNL explain the cryogenic case in more depth.

How Oxygen Sensors Work

Electrochemical (galvanic)

  • A lead anode oxidises as oxygen reaches it
  • Life is finite and set by the oxygen it sees
  • Typical life about 2 years in industrial use
  • Lead free versions now exist as drop in replacements

Zirconia and paramagnetic

  • Zirconia cell runs at about 700 °C on the Nernst principle
  • Zirconia uses ambient air as reference gas
  • Paramagnetic suits percentage levels
  • Paramagnetic is sensitive to vibration

An explanation of oxygen sensor chemistry from AIHA notes that lead free designs avoid lead because of EU restrictions. It also explains that capillary limited sensors read about 20.9% at any altitude, while permeation limited sensors read lower as altitude rises. Our guide to types of gas sensors compares electrochemical cells with the other sensing methods.

Operating conditions also matter for oxygen deficiency monitoring, because they shift the reading. Honeywell's note on operating conditions for oxygen sensors says a sudden pressure rise gives a brief high reading and a drop gives a low one. At 40 °C, the reading in air falls from 20.9% at 0% relative humidity to 19.4% at 100%, which is below a 19.5% alarm. Temperatures above 100 °C damage the sensor permanently.

Alarms, Calibration and Bump Tests

  1. Set the alarms. NIH sets alarms at the OSHA limits, below 19.5% or above 23.5%, and allows caution, warning and alarm stages.
  2. Calibrate the span in fresh air. MSA spans the oxygen sensor to 20.8% in fresh air during zero calibration; other makers use about 20.9%.
  3. Zero with nitrogen. Honeywell's VA201T procedure says the zero gas must be nitrogen.
  4. Bump test portables. OSHA describes a bump test as a check in which challenge gas is passed over the sensor; ISEA advises a check before each day's use.
  5. Calibrate fixed monitors yearly. NIH calls for calibration every 12 months or as the maker says, whichever is more frequent.
  6. Replace sensors on time. Plan for about 2 years of sensor life under typical conditions.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Adding oxygen to sweeten the air. UK HSE says never do this. Ventilate with air and test again.
Testing only near the entrance. UK HSE says test before entry, including the deepest areas where heavy gases collect.
Mounting every sensor at head height. Argon and CO2 collect near the floor, so mount those sensors about 30 cm up.

Frequently Asked Questions

What oxygen level is unsafe?

OSHA treats anything below 19.5% by volume as oxygen deficient and above 23.5% as enriched.

How long does an oxygen sensor last?

Typical galvanic sensors last about 2 years under normal industrial conditions.

Why does my oxygen monitor read low on a humid day?

Humidity lowers the reading. Honeywell shows a drop from 20.9% to 19.4% at 40 °C and 100% relative humidity.
