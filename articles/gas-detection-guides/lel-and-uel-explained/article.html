Key takeaways

  • The LEL is the lowest concentration of a gas in air that can ignite; the UEL is the highest.
  • Between the two limits lies the flammable range, for example 5 to 15 percent by volume for methane.
  • Gas detectors usually read in %LEL, so 100 percent LEL means the gas has reached its lower explosive limit.
  • Temperature, pressure and oxygen level all change the flammable range.

Every flammable gas or vapour can only burn when it is mixed with air in the right proportion. The LEL and UEL, the lower and upper explosive limits, mark the edges of that proportion. They are the foundation of combustible gas detection, hot work permits and confined space entry.

What LEL and UEL Mean

Combustion needs fuel, oxygen and an ignition source, and the fuel and oxygen must be mixed in the right proportion. The lower explosive limit (LEL) is the lowest concentration of a gas in air that will ignite. Below it, the mixture is too lean. The upper explosive limit (UEL) is the highest concentration that will ignite. Above it, the mixture is too rich, but it can become flammable again as it mixes with fresh air.

The same limits are also called the lower and upper flammable limits (LFL and UFL), and the terms are often used interchangeably. For example, methane can only ignite when its concentration in air is between 5 and 15 percent.

%LEL vs %vol

A combustible gas detector rarely shows percent by volume. Instead it shows the concentration as a fraction of the LEL. At 100 percent LEL the gas has reached its lower explosive limit. The conversion is simple: percent by volume equals %LEL divided by 100, multiplied by the LEL of that gas.

For example, 25 percent LEL of methane is 0.25 multiplied by 5, which is 1.25 percent by volume. Percent by volume also converts to ppm by multiplying by 10,000, so the methane LEL equals 50,000 ppm. Did you know?

A very rich atmosphere is not a safe atmosphere. Above the UEL the mixture is too rich to burn, but as fresh air leaks in, it passes back through the flammable range. In addition, catalytic sensors need oxygen to work, so readings in gas rich, oxygen poor air can be misleading. Never assume a very rich atmosphere is safe.

How Gas Detectors Use LEL and UEL

Fixed detectors usually transmit 0 to 100 percent LEL as a 4 to 20 mA signal to a control panel or PLC. Alarm points sit far below the LEL to give people time to react. Published guidance commonly uses a first alarm at 10 percent LEL as a warning and a second alarm at 20 percent LEL for evacuation, while some suppliers describe 25 percent LEL as a standard alarm point.

The low alarm typically starts ventilation and alerts staff, and the high alarm triggers shutdown, isolation or evacuation. In addition, work permits set their own gas test limits, such as below 10 percent LEL for confined space entry.

What Changes the LEL and UEL

Temperature: as temperature rises, the LEL tends to fall and the UEL tends to rise, so the range widens.
Pressure: higher pressure often widens the flammable range.
Oxygen enrichment: lowers the LEL and raises the UEL significantly.
Oxygen depletion and inerting: narrows the range; nitrogen or carbon dioxide inerting is used to prevent explosions.

Ventilation dilutes leaks and keeps them below the LEL. Common Mistakes to Avoid

Reading %LEL as %vol. 50 percent LEL of methane is only 2.5 percent by volume, but it is already half way to ignition.
Forgetting the oxygen level. Catalytic sensors need oxygen, and oxygen enrichment widens the flammable range.
Assuming below 10 percent LEL is always safe. Stratification, ventilation changes and poor sensor placement can hide pockets of gas.
Using an infrared sensor for hydrogen. Most infrared sensors cannot see hydrogen; choose catalytic or another suitable technology.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the LEL of methane?

About 5 percent by volume in air, and its UEL is about 15 percent. So 100 percent LEL on a methane detector equals about 5 percent methane.

What is the difference between LEL and UEL?

The LEL is the lowest concentration that can ignite and the UEL is the highest. Between them is the flammable range.

Why do detectors alarm far below the LEL?

Low alarm points, such as 10 or 20 percent LEL, give people time to ventilate, isolate the leak or evacuate before the mixture can ignite.
