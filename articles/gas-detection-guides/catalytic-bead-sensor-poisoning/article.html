Key takeaways

  • A catalytic bead sensor burns gas on a heated catalyst and measures the heat as a change in resistance in a bridge circuit.
  • Poisons such as silicones cause a permanent loss of sensitivity; inhibitors such as halogenated solvents depress readings, and the sensor usually recovers in clean air.
  • Just a few ppm of silicone vapour can degrade the sensor.
  • A recommended filter, changed weekly, plus testing before use, are the main defences.

A catalytic bead sensor is the classic way to measure flammable gas as a percentage of the LEL. Some chemicals that are common on industrial sites can reduce its sensitivity, partly or completely. SWME supplies fixed and portable gas detectors across the UAE.

How a Catalytic Bead Sensor Works

Honeywell's technical note on handling LEL sensor poisons describes a matched detector and compensator, each a coil of platinum wire embedded in a catalytic bead. When combustible gas oxidises, the heat changes the resistance of one element relative to the other. The bridge circuit detects this as an out of balance voltage.

The sensor needs oxygen to detect gas, as JJS Technical Services points out in its notes on the limitations of LEL sensors. Our guide to gas sensor types compares it with infrared and other sensors, and LEL and UEL explained covers what the reading means.

Poisons and Inhibitors

Honeywell separates the two: poisoning is a permanent degradation, while inhibition is usually recoverable by exposure to clean air. It adds that silicones are usually counted as a poison and H2S as an inhibitor, but most compounds fall into both categories to some degree.

CompoundEffectSource
Silicones, silanes, silicatesThe most serious poisons; a few ppm degrade performanceHoneywell
Tetraethyl leadReduces sensitivity, especially for methane and other gases with high ignition temperaturesHoneywell
Phosphate esters, phosphorus compoundsPoison; oxidised to mineral acids that may corrode the sensorHoneywell, JJS
H2S and other sulfur compoundsInhibitor and poison; corrosive byproducts can damage the bead permanentlyJJS
Halogenated hydrocarbons (Freons, trichloroethylene, methylene chloride)Inhibitor; depresses readings while present, mostly recovers in fresh airJJS
High concentrations of combustible gasDamages the sensorJJS

JJS explains that whether H2S does lasting harm depends on how fast the bead cooks off its byproducts. That in turn depends on bead operating temperature, bead size and whether the bead is powered continuously or pulsed. In addition, Honeywell notes that high concentrations of halogenated hydrocarbons break down to HCl on the bead, which can corrode it and give low readings.

Did you know?

Silicone resistant sensors carry an external filter that removes most silicone vapour before it reaches the bead. JJS notes the trade off: the heavier the hydrocarbon, the more the filter reduces the sensor's response to it.

Where Poisons Come From

Silicone sources

  • Lubricants and mould release agents.
  • Polishes, adhesives and hand creams.
  • Silicone rubber, caulk and sealants.

Inhibitor and corrosive sources

  • Chlorinated solvents.
  • PVC insulated wire overheated during soldering.
  • Hot organic acids such as acetic acid, and acid gases.

How to Prevent Poisoning

{{infographic}}
  1. Use the recommended filter. Honeywell says to always fit one and change it weekly, or immediately after exposure to a poison.
  2. Keep silicones out of the workshop. Do not use silicone polishes, cleaners, lubricants or hand creams where instruments are assembled, tested or stored, and never use silicone lubricated gas valves or regulators with LEL sensors.
  3. Use the maker's spare parts. Silicone gaskets, potting, self stick labels and some moulded plastics can carry poisons into the monitor.
  4. Limit exposure. Turn portable monitors off when not in use, and in poison environments reduce the flow over the sensor or use a diffusion monitor.
  5. Test before use. Honeywell says to always test gas monitors before use, and to clean the pump and replace tubing, filter and gasket after serious exposure.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Spraying silicone lubricant near detectors. A few ppm of silicone vapour is enough to degrade a catalytic bead sensor.
Leaving the same filter in for months. Honeywell's guidance is weekly, or straight after poison exposure.
Assuming a low reading means no gas. A poisoned or inhibited sensor reads low. Test it with gas before you trust it.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between poisoning and inhibition?

Poisoning permanently degrades the sensor. Inhibition depresses the reading while the compound is present, and the sensor usually recovers in clean air.

What is the worst poison for a catalytic bead sensor?

Silicones. Honeywell calls silicon compounds the most serious poisons.

Does a catalytic bead sensor need oxygen?

Yes. It needs oxygen to burn the gas on the bead.
