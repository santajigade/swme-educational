Key takeaways

  • Hydrogen sulfide is toxic, flammable and heavier than air, so it collects in low lying areas.
  • At about 100 ppm it can deaden the sense of smell within minutes, so odour is not a warning.
  • OSHA sets a 20 ppm ceiling for general industry; NIOSH sets 100 ppm as immediately dangerous to life and health.

Hydrogen sulfide is one of the most common hazards in oil and gas production, wastewater and chemical plants. It smells of rotten eggs at low levels, which gives a false sense of safety, because higher concentrations destroy the sense of smell. Reliable H2S gas detection with instruments is the only dependable warning.

Why H2S Is So Dangerous

OSHA describes hydrogen sulfide as a highly flammable, explosive gas with an explosive range in air of 4.3 to 45 percent. Workers are exposed mainly by breathing it. The odour threshold is 0.01 to 1.5 ppm, but at 100 ppm OSHA reports loss of smell after 2 to 15 minutes. Emerson adds that H2S is heavier than air and sinks to the lowest lying area.

ConcentrationEffect reported by OSHA
2 to 5 ppmProlonged exposure may cause nausea, tearing eyes and headaches
20 ppmPossible fatigue, headache, irritability, poor memory and dizziness
100 ppmCoughing, eye irritation and loss of smell after 2 to 15 minutes
500 to 700 ppmStaggering and collapse in 5 minutes; death after 30 to 60 minutes
700 to 1,000 ppmRapid unconsciousness within 1 or 2 breaths

Exposure Limits

Limits published by OSHA

OSHA PEL, general industry: 20 ppm ceiling, with a 50 ppm peak allowed for up to 10 minutes if there is no other exposure during the shift.
OSHA PEL, construction and shipyards: 10 ppm as an 8 hour average.
NIOSH REL: 10 ppm as a 10 minute ceiling.
NIOSH IDLH: 100 ppm, immediately dangerous to life and health.

Local rules can be stricter. For example, Emerson quotes UK HSE limits of 5 ppm over 8 hours and 10 ppm over 15 minutes. Set alarm levels to your site procedure and the applicable regulation. The flammable range sits far above the toxic levels; for flammable gas basics read LEL and UEL explained.

Sensors for H2S Gas Detection

Emerson compares the main technologies used for H2S gas detection. Our guide to types of gas sensors covers how each one works.

SensorStrengthsLimitations
ElectrochemicalHigh sensitivity and repeatability; T90 about 30 seconds in temperate conditionsAffected by heat, dryness and humidity; slows near minus 20 degrees C; calibration about every six months
Metal oxide semiconductor (MOS)Long life; works well at high temperature and in very dry airT90 up to 120 seconds for some makers; can stop responding until bump tested
Open path opticalInherently fail safe; covers a line across an areaCannot give an accurate ppm value; needs a clear beam

Did you know?

Electrochemical H2S sensors can dry out in hot, dry climates. Emerson notes that electrolyte evaporation in these conditions shortens sensor life, which matters for outdoor detectors in the Gulf summer.

Where to Place Detectors

{{infographic}}

Good H2S gas detection depends on placement as much as on the sensor. Because H2S sinks, sensing elements must be placed to reflect that tendency. Mount fixed detectors low, close to likely leak points and wherever gas could collect. Draeger recommends fixed, continuous monitoring for long production phases and personal monitors for field work. For mounting heights across gas types, see our gas detector placement guide.

Fixed detectors

  • Continuous area monitoring
  • Mounted low near leak sources
  • Alert at preset set points
  • Suit long production phases

Portable monitors

  • Worn by the individual worker
  • Used during field inspection
  • Single gas or multigas units
  • Follow the same test routine

Good Practice Checklist

  1. Know the source. Map where H2S can be released and where it can collect.
  2. Mount low. Place sensors near ground level and in low points.
  3. Match the climate. Choose a sensor rated for local heat and humidity.
  4. Test and calibrate. Follow a routine; see bump test vs calibration.
  5. Record results. Log alarms, tests and calibrations for each detector.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Relying on smell. Lethal concentrations cannot be detected by nose, as Draeger warns.
Mounting detectors high. H2S is heavier than air and collects low.
Stretching calibration intervals. Drift and dry out go unnoticed without routine checks.

Frequently Asked Questions

At what level is H2S dangerous?

NIOSH lists 100 ppm as immediately dangerous to life and health, and OSHA sets a 20 ppm ceiling for general industry.

Where should H2S detectors be mounted?

Low, near leak sources and low points, because hydrogen sulfide is heavier than air.

Which sensor is best for H2S gas detection?

Electrochemical sensors are the usual choice for toxic H2S; MOS can suit very hot, dry sites.
